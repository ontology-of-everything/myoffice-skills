# Talk Outline Structure Patterns

Use this to select the right structural skeleton for the talk before building the outline. The pattern should fit the talk's angle, the theory/practical balance, and the session length.

---

## Pattern 1: Problem → Diagnosis → Solution

**Best for:** Talks introducing a fix, tool, or method for a widely-felt problem. Strong for practical-heavy talks.

**Structure:**
1. Open with the problem — make the audience feel it, not just understand it
2. Diagnose why the obvious/current approach fails
3. Introduce the solution
4. Show it working (demo or case study)
5. Address tradeoffs honestly
6. Close with what to do next

**Watch for:** Spending too long on the problem. The diagnosis phase often eats the demo time. Keep problem+diagnosis to 30% of the slot at most.

---

## Pattern 2: Narrative / Case Study

**Best for:** Talks built around a real incident, migration, or project. Works at any theory/practical balance.

**Structure:**
1. Set the scene — who, what, when
2. The inciting event or challenge
3. What the team tried (including what failed)
4. What worked and why
5. What they'd do differently
6. What the audience should take from it

**Watch for:** Getting lost in the narrative and running out of time for the takeaway. The "what the audience should take from it" section gets cut when talks run long — but it's the reason anyone is there.

---

## Pattern 3: Tutorial / Walkthrough

**Best for:** Highly practical talks where the audience will follow along or want to replicate the steps. Workshop formats.

**Structure:**
1. What we're building and why (2–3 min max)
2. Setup and prerequisites check
3. Step 1 → demo/code
4. Step 2 → demo/code
5. Step N → demo/code
6. The finished thing — what it looks like end-to-end
7. What to explore next / where to go from here

**Watch for:** Tutorial talks that don't explain the "why" behind each step. If every step is just "do this", the talk loses anyone who hits a problem. Build in one-sentence explanations at each step.

**Demo note:** Tutorial talks have the highest demo risk of any format. Every step is a live dependency. Always have a "resume from here" checkpoint for each major step — a branch, a snapshot, a known-good state the speaker can jump to if a step fails.

---

## Pattern 4: Argument / Thesis

**Best for:** Opinionated, theory-heavy talks making a case for changing how people think or work. Keynotes, conference talks at practitioner events.

**Structure:**
1. State the thesis early — don't bury the lede
2. Acknowledge the prevailing view (steelman it, don't strawman)
3. First piece of evidence or argument
4. Second piece of evidence or argument
5. Third piece of evidence or argument (optional — don't over-stack)
6. Address the strongest counterargument directly
7. Restate the thesis with more weight behind it
8. What this means practically — give the audience something to do

**Watch for:** Talks that argue convincingly but don't land on anything actionable. An audience that agrees but doesn't know what to change leaves feeling vaguely inspired and does nothing.

---

## Pattern 5: Concept Introduction

**Best for:** Talks introducing something new — a technology, pattern, or idea — to an audience encountering it for the first time. Balanced theory/practical.

**Structure:**
1. The problem this thing addresses (audience must feel this before they care about the solution)
2. What this thing is — the mental model
3. How it works — just enough detail to make the mental model concrete
4. A simple demo or example
5. A more realistic or complex example
6. When to use it and when not to
7. Where to go to learn more

**Watch for:** Concept introduction talks that go too deep too fast. The first demo should be almost embarrassingly simple. Complexity in the second example is fine — but if example one requires setup explanation, it's too complex.

---

## Pattern 6: Comparison / Decision Guide

**Best for:** Talks helping an audience choose between options. Common at events where the audience is in evaluation mode.

**Structure:**
1. The decision context — why this choice matters
2. The options (introduce all of them before going deep on any)
3. Option A: what it is, when it wins, where it struggles
4. Option B: same
5. Option C (if applicable): same
6. The decision framework — what questions to ask to pick the right one
7. Recommendation (be willing to make one — "it depends" is a non-answer)

**Watch for:** Comparison talks that are secretly ads for one option. Audiences spot this and disengage. If the speaker has a clear preference, state it explicitly with reasoning rather than pretending to be neutral.

---

## Timing guides by length

| Slot length | Usable content time (after open/close buffers) | Max sections | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 min | ~16 min | 3–4 | One demo max. No interaction. Tight. |
| 30 min | ~26 min | 4–5 | One or two demos. One interaction moment. |
| 45 min | ~40 min | 5–6 | Two or three demos. Interaction works well. |
| 60 min | ~52 min | 6–7 | Can support a richer demo sequence or a Q&A-heavy close. |
| 90 min | ~80 min | Workshop structure | Needs checkpoint breaks and resume points throughout. |

For talks with Q&A: subtract Q&A time from usable content time before sectioning.
